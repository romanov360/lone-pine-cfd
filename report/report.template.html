Computational fluid dynamics 500 mL glass bottle 40 °C into 10 °C water

Still box or
moving creek?

A warm glass bottle goes into cold water two ways — a box of motionless water, and a flowing creek. The creek wins, essentially always. But the reason is not the one the question implies, and the margin is far smaller than intuition suggests. A brisk current multiplies the outside film coefficient by 3.7× and the actual cooling rate by 1.27×, because the outside of the bottle was never what was holding things up.

Why the creek barely helps

Heat leaving the bottle crosses three resistances in series: the water inside must reach the wall, the wall must conduct, and the outside film must carry it away. A current acts on one of those three — and on a glass bottle it is the smallest one.

The 3 mm glass wall alone is worth an equivalent film coefficient of just 317 W/m²K. Still water already supplies 466. By the time the creek is running at 0.35 m/s the outside film has stopped being a bottleneck at all — it is down to a tenth of the total.

Transient model

The effect that actually dominates: the box fills up

Cooling a 500 mL bottle from 40 °C to 5 °C dumps about 82 kJ into the bath. A 2 L box has a heat capacity of only 8.4 kJ/K, so it warms by more than 6 degrees absorbing that — and the bottle cannot get colder than the water around it. A 2 L box equilibrates at 16.5 °C and never reaches 15 at all, no matter how hard you stir it.

A creek is not just moving. It is unlimited, which for this problem is the more valuable property of the two.

What the simulation adds

Textbook free-convection correlations assume a body hanging in an infinite quiescent medium. A box is not one, and the difference is large enough to matter. Running the real geometry — buoyancy, plume, walls and all — shows a tight box strangling its own convection:

A box is not an infinite medium

The film coefficient plateaus at 445 W/m²K once the side gap exceeds about one bottle diameter — the 26 cm and 40 cm boxes agree to 0.4 % — and collapses to 323 when the gap closes to a third of a diameter. Confinement is the fall below that plateau, and it costs 27 % in a tight tub. The narrow box does two things to itself at once: it recirculates water the bottle has already warmed, and it stratifies, so the warmest water pools at the top, exactly where the bottle is. The separate offset between the plateau and the correlation's 520 is the solver's own 2-D limit at Ra ≈ 109, not confinement — the next table pins that down.

The same five minutes, seen two ways

Water temperature in a vertical slice through the bottle, at matched times and on one colour scale. Top: a closed 24 × 30 cm box. Bottom: an open channel with 2 cm/s flowing left to right. Same bottle, same 1.5 mm mesh, same solver; conjugate glass wall throughout.

In the box, the plume leaves the top of the bottle, spreads under the lid and builds a stable warm layer that grows downward. By five minutes the bottle's upper half is no longer sitting in 10 °C water — it is sitting in 14 °C water it warmed itself. In the channel the same plume is simply carried away: the bath stays at 10 °C everywhere except a thin wake, and the bottle never stops seeing fresh cold water. That is the creek's real advantage, and no correlation written for “an infinite quiescent medium” can represent either half of it.

In open water the correlation is right; in a box it is not

The same CFD and the same bottle, run in a channel instead of a box. With a current the textbook mixed-convection correlation is reproduced almost exactly. Without one it is not — and the first row, which matches the large boxes above rather than sitting above them, is how we know that offset is the solver's own limit rather than confinement.

The agreement then degrades, and by 0.1 m/s the error has changed sign — which is the useful signal. A discretisation error shrinks smoothly; an error that grows, flips, and arrives with large scatter is the two-dimensional representation ceasing to be physical. At Re ≈ 5,000 a real cylinder wake is three-dimensional and transitional, and a 2-D simulation cannot cascade energy the right way. That is exactly why the fast-creek numbers in this study come from experimental correlations rather than from this solver.

Property model

Oxygenation: three questions, three different answers

“Aerated creek water” bundles three physically distinct things together, and they do not agree with each other.

Dissolved oxygen does nothing. A 10 °C creek at full saturation holds 11.3 mg/L — a mass fraction of 1.1 × 10−5. Going from zero to twice saturation moves the film coefficient by −0.001 %. The six standard correlations disagree with each other by ±20 % on the same case, so dissolved oxygen sits four orders of magnitude below the noise floor.

Dissolved gas versus actual bubbles

Bubbles hurt the properties and help by stirring. Real bubbles displace water, and water is what carries the heat — at 5 % void the mixture loses 5 % of its volumetric heat capacity and 7 % of its conductivity, both the wrong direction. What rescues them is motion: a rising swarm drags liquid along at roughly 0.2 m/s. That is the entire benefit of aeration, and it therefore helps exactly where there was no motion to begin with.

Aeration helps still water and hurts fast water

Net change in the film coefficient against entrained air fraction, for four background flow speeds.

Bubbling air into the still box nearly quadruples its film coefficient. Bubbling air into a 1 m/s creek makes it worse — the creek already supplied more velocity than the bubbles can add, so all they do is dilute the water. Aeration narrows the gap between the two setups rather than widening it.

Property model

A trap the still box cannot escape

Water is densest at 3.98 °C, not at freezing. Below that its thermal expansion coefficient is negative and buoyancy runs backwards. When a bottle's surface and the bath straddle that maximum, their densities become equal — and free convection, which runs entirely on density difference, simply stops.

A surface at 6 °C in a 2 °C bath has densities equal to six significant figures. A creek does not care — forced convection has no such hole. Very cold still water underperforms its reputation for exactly this reason.

A note on the algebra

The textbook Grashof number gβΔT L³/ν² returns a negative value here, and the correlations then return NaN. That is not a bug to be clamped away — it is the algebra complaining about real physics. This study uses an exact density-difference form throughout, which reduces to the usual one away from 4 °C and stays valid through it.

Verification & validation

How the solver was checked

The CFD is a purpose-built staggered-grid incompressible Navier–Stokes solver with Boussinesq buoyancy, van Leer TVD advection, a separable Poisson solver and a single-domain conjugate energy equation. None of its results are worth reading unless it reproduces problems whose answers are already known, so it was run against three of them first.

Benchmarks

Each benchmark exercises a different part of the solver, in the order the parts are stacked.

BenchmarkExercisesReferenceResult

The heated-cavity agreement matters most here: it is the same physics as a bottle shedding a buoyant plume into a closed box, and it lands within 0.73 % across three decades of Rayleigh number. The last row is the one honest caveat made quantitative — at water's Prandtl number the thermal boundary layer is thin enough that the wall-flux estimate needs a fine mesh. Refining it four times gives clean first-order convergence (p = 0.99, which is exactly what a staircase boundary should give) and extrapolates to within 2.6 % of the correlation.

Grid convergence was run on the box case at four spacings from 2.0 down to 0.75 mm. The coarsest is clearly under-resolved; the three finest agree to within the run-to-run scatter of the flow itself, which at Ra ≈ 109 is genuinely unsteady. No asymptotic range is established, so no extrapolated value is quoted as though one were. The honest reading is h = 435 ± 10 W/m²K in a 24 cm box.

Two bugs were caught by these benchmarks rather than by inspection, and both are worth naming because both produced plausible-looking numbers. The first: an “isothermal” body was approximated by giving it a large conductivity and a very large heat capacity — which divide, leaving its diffusivity a thousand times below the surrounding water, so its surface cooled and every film coefficient came out low. The giveaway was a Nusselt number that fell with Reynolds number. The second: a thin glass wall cannot be held by any immersed-boundary treatment at two or three cells thick; hard masking destroyed the divergence-free condition and penalisation leaked the bottle's contents into the bath in twenty seconds. Both were found by energy-conservation and benchmark checks, which is what those checks are for.

Limits

What this does not settle

  • The CFD is two-dimensional. That is the right call for plume structure, confinement and stratification, but a real creek at Re ≈ 23,000 is three-dimensional and turbulent. Fast-creek film coefficients come from correlations; the CFD covers the range where a 2-D simulation is defensible and checks them there.
  • Circulation inside the bottle is modelled, not resolved. A 3 mm glass wall is two or three cells thick, and no immersed-boundary treatment holds a membrane that thin without either destroying the divergence-free condition or leaking the bottle's contents into the bath. The interior is carried as an effective conductivity instead. Whether the water inside circulates is worth a factor of three — more than the entire box-versus-creek question — and it is the largest single uncertainty here.
  • The bottle is assumed submerged and still. A bottle actually wedged in a creek also gets held in continuously fresh water, which is the infinite-reservoir effect again, by another route.
  • Correlation spread is ±20 %. Six standard forced-convection correlations were carried in parallel throughout rather than picking one; where they disagree, that disagreement is shown rather than averaged away.
